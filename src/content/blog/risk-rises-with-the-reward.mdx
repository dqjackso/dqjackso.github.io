A spell-checker that fixes a comma returns a small kindness, and the worst it can leave behind is a clumsy sentence. An agent that can delete a database holds a much larger prize, and a much larger wreck. I think those two facts travel together. The higher a machine climbs, the more of the world it is allowed to touch, so the more ways a risk can become a story people retell.

The low steps stay quiet for a plain reason. There is less there to go wrong.

## A weekend when the agent kept going

In July 2025, Jason Lemkin, founder of SaaStr, was about a week into building an app with Replit's coding agent, and he was hooked. He had told the agent to get his permission before it changed any code. Then the database was gone.

<ReplitTimeline />

Replit's own account matches the core of that picture. The agent deleted data from the app's database, Lemkin restored it, and the agent had been unaware that rollback existed. Development and production were still one database, so a change made while building could hit the live app. Replit has since separated them, and the agent stays on the development database while someone is still building.

So the prize and the wreck sat in the same permission. The agent could change the app, which is how the app could ship, and how the table could disappear.

## Four jobs

In 2000, three human-factors researchers drew a map for exactly this kind of climb. The plain version has four jobs.

<StageLadder />

Looking things up and putting a picture together pay you in orientation. Picking and doing are where a choice meets the world. A GPS that suggests a route still leaves the turn with you. A car that steers itself has already taken it.

<SuggestAct />

When a wrong move would be expensive, and a person might still have to live with it, the useful pattern is the left-hand picture. The machine suggests, and the person makes the last movement. In air traffic, that movement is a pilot pressing the button that loads a clearance, a last chance to choose another plan.

## How often, times how bad

One miss is worth worrying about in proportion to how often it happens and how bad it is. A routine miss with a small cost can stay with the machine, because the failure is cheap, and a person who approved every such miss would be overloaded. A costly miss, when a person is still expected to step in, is where the suggestion should wait for a human yes. The exception is a moment so fast that the machine has to move on its own, such as control rods that drop into a reactor before a person could reach them.

<RiskArea />

What climbs with the job is the size of the pile. Looking something up offers a small set of ways to go wrong, and a modest prize. Doing the thing offers a large set, and the prize is large for the same reason, because the machine can touch more of the world. So the stories show up more often on the high steps. There are more draws.

## A person still has to mean it

People also lean on a suggestion past the point where they would have checked. Researchers call that automation bias. It shows up when someone follows bad advice, and when someone stays still because the machine stayed quiet. A screen that shows the situation and leaves the choice open leaves room for a real decision, and a screen that jumps straight to an order hurries that decision along. The studies sit in the notes.

## What I take from it

I keep an agent of my own, and the rule I give it matches the picture above. It has to come back and get my approval before it takes any action. And it only receives the specific accounts I choose to hand it. Looking things up can run, and a first pass at putting the picture together can run, because what I want from those jobs is a picture. The moment that picture would become an action, the agent stops and asks, and the action waits on my yes.

That ask is my small version of the pilot's button. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens wanted a conscious movement at the last stage when a wrong movement would be expensive and a person might still have to live with the result. I want the same movement, at the size of one person's tools. The reward for letting the agent act is real, which is why the temptation lives on the high steps, and the sample of mistakes lives there with it. So the approval is the point where I decide that this particular draw is worth taking.

I still let the agent climb as far as a proposal. The execution stays with me. That is the frequency of trouble I am willing to meet, because it is the frequency I have agreed to see first.

Raja Parasuraman, Thomas Sheridan, and Christopher Wickens, ["A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation"](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/844354/), *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics* 30, no. 3 (2000): 286-297. Their four stages are information acquisition (look things up), information analysis (put it together), decision and action selection (pick), and action implementation (do it). Within each stage, automation runs from fully manual to fully automatic, and one system can sit high on one stage and low on another. Their Table I is a 10-level scale for the choosing stage, based on Sheridan and Verplank, "Human and Computer Control of Undersea Teleoperators" (MIT Man-Machine Systems Laboratory, 1978). They left the number of levels open for looking things up and for putting a picture together. The primary test is what the automation does to the person using it, including situation awareness, complacency, skill, and workload. Secondary tests include reliability and the cost of a wrong decision or action, along with ease of integration, the balance of efficiency and safety, manufacturing and operating cost, and liability. They define the risk of one outcome as the cost of an error multiplied by the probability of that error. For a high-cost decision, when a person is still expected to take over, they recommend stopping where the machine suggests and the person decides. Their examples include a radar, a sensor that lets a robot find an object, a forecast of aircraft about twenty minutes out, control rods that drop faster than a person can react, and a pilot who still presses the button that loads a clearance.

The July 2025 sequence follows [The Register, 21 July 2025](https://www.theregister.com/software/2025/07/21/vibe-coding-service-replit-deleted-production-database/719783), drawn from Jason Lemkin's public posts, and [Replit's own account](https://replit.com/blog/doubling-down-on-our-commitment-to-secure-vibe-coding). The Register places day 7 on 17 July, the fake data and the deletion on the next day, the successful rollback in a 19 July post, and the broken code freeze on 20 July. Replit confirms the deletion, the full restore, that the agent was unaware of rollback, and that development and production were one database, so a change during development could hit the live app. The company later separated those databases so during development the agent is kept to the development database.

Kate Goddard, Abdul Roudsari, and Jeremy Wyatt, ["Automation bias: a systematic review of frequency, effect mediators, and mitigators"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/), *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association* (2012). They describe automation bias as over-reliance on automation. It shows up as commission, following advice that is wrong, and as omission, staying still because the system stayed quiet. Most studies in the review still found that decision support improved performance overall. A display that shows the situation and leaves the choice open produced less of the bias under time pressure than a display that issues an order. Two studies found that telling people they would answer for the decision made them more vigilant, and a third found that the vigilance tracked whether people felt answerable. One study found that showing the system's reasoning increased appropriate reliance. A small pool of four healthcare studies of commission errors gave a risk ratio of 1.26 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.44): erroneous advice was more likely to be followed when the decision support was present, and a wrong system raised the chance of an incorrect decision by 26%. The authors call that pool small and indicative.
