Every October, my son wants the Halloween animatronics. He loves them. He knows the scare is coming. He still jumps, every time. And he loves that feeling.

I remember watching the order of it. [[CONFIRM: where you were, which animatronic it was, and how he could tell it was about to go.]] He already knew. His body went anyway. And then he was glad. That gladness is what sent me after the science.

Scientists have been firing guns near people for almost a century, trying to find out why the jump still comes.

## The gun you were warned about

In 1939, the psychologists Carney Landis and William Hunt published *The Startle Pattern*, a catalog of the full-body flinch people make at a gunshot. In a [1985 follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4078682/), Paul Ekman, Wallace Friesen, and Ronald Simons fired a blank pistol near volunteers, sometimes by surprise and sometimes after telling them exactly when it would go off. They also asked some people to hold perfectly still. According to [a later summary by researchers at Berkeley and Stanford](https://bpl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/docs/92-Expressive%20suppression06.pdf), Ekman's team concluded that the startle is shown by every subject, is easier to elicit than an emotion, still breaks through when people try to hold still, and looks wrong when people try to act it. People who tried to stifle it looked about the same in the first half second, a little weaker.

And then Tim Hagemann, Robert Levenson, and James Gross picked up the same thread. They blasted 252 people with a loud noise. Two groups watched a 20-second countdown first, and one of those groups was told to hide any reaction. The people who tried to suppress their startle showed *more* sympathetic nervous system activation. Keeping a straight face, it seems, costs the body something.

## Your brainstem gets there first

The reason is speed. The core startle reflex runs through a tiny brainstem circuit. In rats, Michael Davis and colleagues [measured it at about 8 milliseconds](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/2/6/791) from the sound to the first twitch in a hind leg, a latency so short that only a handful of synapses can be involved. In humans, researchers measure startle as an eyeblink, and [published guidelines](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-8986.2005.00271.x) score adult blinks that begin roughly 20 to 80 milliseconds after a loud sound. Conscious recognition comes later; an [Open University course](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/health-sports-psychology/health/emotions-and-emotional-disorders/content-section-2.4) puts it at roughly 200 milliseconds, a rough teaching estimate.

Those three times sit on one clock.

The neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux gave this gap its most famous framing. Sensory information can take a "low road" from the thalamus straight to the amygdala, which is fast and crude, or a "high road" through the cortex, which is slower but detailed enough to tell you the thump on the porch was the cat. And LeDoux himself has since sharpened the story in a way that matters here. He now argues that the amygdala [detects threats and triggers the body's defensive responses](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)30492-9), while the conscious *feeling* of fear is assembled separately in cortical networks. With psychiatrist Daniel Pine, he calls this a [two-system framework](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16030353).

So the flinch and the feeling run on different machinery, which is why my son can love an animatronic and still jump when it goes.

And then came the part that surprised me. Knowing a scare is coming can make the flinch bigger. Davis, Christian Grillon, and colleagues have spent years showing that the amygdala and the neighboring bed nucleus of the stria terminalis [turn up the gain on the startle reflex](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2795099/) when an animal or person is anticipating a threat. Your expectation turns that reflex up.

## When matters more than whether

And the kind of uncertainty matters. In Grillon's [NPU-threat test](https://www.nature.com/articles/nprot.2012.001), people sit through safe periods, periods where a signal warns them exactly when an unpleasant stimulus will come, and periods where it can come at any moment. The unpredictable periods produce bigger startles.

The test has three rooms.

A 2018 study by Ken Bennett, Jacqueline Dickmann, and Christine Larson at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee [pulled the two kinds of uncertainty apart](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6013348/). Volunteers watched a loading bar while waiting for a possible shock. In one condition the shock was certain but its timing was random. In another, the timing was fixed but the shock came only half the time. Uncertainty about *when* produced the strongest startle. But the volunteers said both kinds of uncertainty felt about the same. So the blink had a ranking, and the story they told about the feeling had a tie.

A [2016 study](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms10996) by Archy de Berker and colleagues at University College London adds a wrinkle. People's stress, pupil size, and skin conductance tracked how uncertain the next shock was. And the people whose stress tracked uncertainty most closely learned the task best, which makes stress look like a tool for paying attention.

## Why we pay for it

People pay to be frightened on purpose, and the best evidence for why comes from inside haunted houses. In a [2020 field study in *Psychological Science*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734554/), researchers at Aarhus University's Recreational Fear Lab strapped heart-rate monitors on 110 visitors to a haunted attraction. Enjoyment peaked at a moderate level of fear and fell off toward either end. Most participants described the experience as a kind of play.

The shape they describe is the old inverted U.

Sociologist Margee Kerr and colleagues [surveyed and recorded EEG from visitors](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_a_haunted_house_can_make_you_happier) to an adults-only Pittsburgh haunt and found that people left in a better mood, especially those who arrived bored, tired, or stressed. Their [paper in *Emotion*](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000470) argues that fear experienced "within the context of safety and control" offers a chance to practice tolerating distress. These visitors chose to be there, so the pattern describes people who walked in on purpose, and that pattern is striking.

## What I take from it

In Army Special Operations intelligence, my body got there first. So I learned to give the slower part of me a beat before I decided what it was.

I archery hunt elk in Arizona. In 2026 I hunted unit 7E and came home empty-handed. Coming home empty-handed was on the table the whole time. The heavier part was the unnamed minute. [[CONFIRM: a sensory detail from a day in unit 7E, the cold, the light, a sound you waited on.]] A named time to walk out gives that wait an edge. And the waiting eases.

From 2019 to August 2026 I ran my former company. [[CONFIRM: one time you hid a flinch while you were running it, who was there, and what your body did afterward.]] Hagemann's volunteers paid for a straight face after a loud noise and a countdown. I recognize that cost. I consult at SEI now. When the flinch comes, I want to let it show, at least to myself.

And then there is my son. A moderate scare, chosen on purpose, is practice. He knows the jump is coming. He still jumps. And he loves the feeling. When I watch him, I see the middle the haunt studies describe.

So this October I will watch him again. He will know the scare is coming. He will jump. And he will love it. His body goes first, and the gladness follows. That gap is old. And the jump that comes out of it is the feeling he loves.
