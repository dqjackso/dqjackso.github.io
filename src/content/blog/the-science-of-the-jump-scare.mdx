My son Carter is nine, and he likes to stand in front of an animatronic named Trixie, giddy, as if a burlap bag and a surprise knife were a treat. She holds the bag, pops up with the knife, and then the mask on her face pops up to reveal a creepy smile. He knows that sequence exactly, and he still jumps when she runs it, and the feeling of the jump is the part he loves.

But here is the odd part, which is why the rest of this exists. Carter already holds the whole warning, and his body jumps anyway, and the jump is what he loves. So the warning and the flinch are in on it together. Scientists have been firing guns near people for almost a century, trying to find out how a partnership like that can last.

## The gun you were warned about

In 1939, the psychologists Carney Landis and William Hunt published *The Startle Pattern*, a catalog of the full-body flinch people make at a gunshot. In a [1985 follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4078682/), Paul Ekman, Wallace Friesen, and Ronald Simons fired a blank pistol near volunteers, sometimes by surprise and sometimes after telling them exactly when it would go off. They also asked some people to hold perfectly still. According to [a later summary by researchers at Berkeley and Stanford](https://bpl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/docs/92-Expressive%20suppression06.pdf), Ekman's team concluded that the startle is shown by every subject, is easier to elicit than an emotion, still breaks through when people try to hold still, and looks wrong when people try to act it. People who tried to stifle it looked about the same in the first half second, a little weaker.

That later study, by Tim Hagemann, Robert Levenson, and James Gross, added a twist I find delightful. They blasted 252 people with a loud noise. Two groups watched a 20-second countdown first, and one of those groups was told to hide any reaction. The people who tried to suppress their startle showed *more* sympathetic nervous system activation. Keeping a straight face, it seems, costs the body something.

## Your brainstem gets there first

The explanation is mostly speed. The core startle reflex runs through a tiny brainstem circuit. In rats, Michael Davis and colleagues [measured it at about 8 milliseconds](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/2/6/791) from the sound to the first twitch in a hind leg, a latency so short that only a handful of synapses can be involved. In humans, researchers measure startle as an eyeblink, and [published guidelines](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-8986.2005.00271.x) score adult blinks that begin roughly 20 to 80 milliseconds after a loud sound. Conscious recognition comes later; an [Open University course](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/health-sports-psychology/health/emotions-and-emotional-disorders/content-section-2.4) puts it at roughly 200 milliseconds, a rough teaching estimate.

The neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux gave this gap its most famous framing. Sensory information can take a "low road" from the thalamus straight to the amygdala, which is fast and crude, or a "high road" through the cortex, which is slower but detailed enough to tell you the thump on the porch was the cat. And LeDoux himself has since sharpened the story in a way that matters here. He now argues that the amygdala [detects threats and triggers the body's defensive responses](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)30492-9), while the conscious *feeling* of fear is assembled separately in cortical networks. With psychiatrist Daniel Pine, he calls this a [two-system framework](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16030353).

So the flinch and the feeling run on different machinery, which is why Carter can know Trixie's creepy smile is coming and still jump when she puts it on.

But here is the part that surprised me most. Knowing a scare is coming can make the flinch bigger. Davis, Christian Grillon, and colleagues have spent years showing that the amygdala and the neighboring bed nucleus of the stria terminalis [turn up the gain on the startle reflex](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2795099/) when an animal or person is anticipating a threat. Your expectation turns that reflex up.

## When matters more than whether

And the kind of uncertainty matters. In Grillon's [NPU-threat test](https://www.nature.com/articles/nprot.2012.001), people sit through safe periods, periods where a signal warns them exactly when an unpleasant stimulus will come, and periods where it can come at any moment. The unpredictable periods produce bigger startles.

The test has three rooms.

A 2018 study by Ken Bennett, Jacqueline Dickmann, and Christine Larson at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee [pulled the two kinds of uncertainty apart](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6013348/). Volunteers watched a loading bar while waiting for a possible shock. In one condition the shock was certain but its timing was random. In another, the timing was fixed but the shock came only half the time. Uncertainty about *when* produced the strongest startle. Oddly, people *said* both kinds of uncertainty felt equally nerve-racking, so the body and the self-report told somewhat different stories.

That is exactly the situation in a well-decorated front yard. Every trick-or-treater knows something will happen, and the moment stays a surprise.

A [2016 study](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms10996) by Archy de Berker and colleagues at University College London adds a wrinkle. People's stress, pupil size, and skin conductance tracked how uncertain the next shock was. And the people whose stress tracked uncertainty most closely learned the task best, which makes stress look like a tool for paying attention.

## Why we pay for it

People also buy tickets to be frightened on purpose, and the best evidence for why comes from inside haunted houses. In a [2020 field study in *Psychological Science*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734554/), researchers at Aarhus University's Recreational Fear Lab strapped heart-rate monitors on 110 visitors to a haunted attraction. Enjoyment peaked at a moderate level of fear and fell off toward either end. Most participants described the experience as a kind of play.

The shape they describe is the old inverted U.

Sociologist Margee Kerr and colleagues [surveyed and recorded EEG from visitors](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_a_haunted_house_can_make_you_happier) to an adults-only Pittsburgh haunt and found that people left in a better mood, especially those who arrived bored, tired, or stressed. Their [paper in *Emotion*](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000470) argues that fear experienced "within the context of safety and control" offers a chance to practice tolerating distress. These visitors chose to be there, so the pattern describes people who walked in on purpose, and that pattern is striking.

## What I take from it

In Army Special Operations intelligence, I watched ISIS propaganda footage showing many deaths. I will leave the images there. My body registered them and carried them, and I understood the trauma many years later. The researchers in this essay clock the gap between a flinch and a mind in milliseconds. The gap I lived through lasted years.

I archery hunt elk in Arizona, and in 2026 I sat in unit 7E in the fog, the forest dead quiet, while icy raindrops pitter-pattered around me. It felt like being insulated from the rest of the world, just me and Mother Nature. I had chosen that uncertainty on purpose, a moment that might arrive while I waited inside the quiet, and the peace was in the waiting. I went home empty-handed, and the peace of that wait came with me.

During a crisis at my former company, the investors were extremely angry and threatening me, and the employees were anxious and scared of losing their jobs. I had to make the decisions and find a way forward, and I had to reassure people and answer their questions with a calm face. Hagemann's volunteers paid in sympathetic activation for a straight face after a countdown and a loud noise. Through that crisis I was paying a bill of the same kind, because the calm had a cost and my body was the one covering it.

And then there is Carter and Trixie, which is the light version of a lesson he is already practicing. A moderate scare, chosen on purpose, sits at the top of the inverted U the haunt studies describe, where enjoyment peaks and then falls away toward either end. Trixie is enough knife and enough smile to get the jump, and Carter knows her well enough to stand there giddy and love the feeling when it lands. So this Halloween, when the mask pops up, his body will go first and the joy will be right behind it, a nine-year-old finding the middle of the curve. It is the best argument I know for a creepy smile.
